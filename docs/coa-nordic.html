COA and eCOA in Denmark and the Nordics

The same electronic assessment crosses five national systems when a study recruits in the Nordic countries. Under the EU clinical trials regulation the route is common, through one portal. What differs is the language of what participants see, which body reads it, and which approved language versions of an instrument exist. This page sets out what the authorities say, and where nothing could be found.

Reference · sources checked 2026-11-14

Boundary. A reading of published authority pages, not legal advice and not a substitute for asking the authority or ethics committee. Several national pages could not be read (Sweden's medicines agency and ethical review authority, Finland's ethics committee, Iceland's medicines agency) and are marked as open questions below. Anything labelled inference is ClinicOps's reading, not the authority's. Finnish and Icelandic material needs a native-language reviewer in every case.

What is common across the Nordics

  • One route. Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Norway and Iceland all use the EU clinical trials information system for applications. Norway and Iceland take part as EEA states. The Danish, Norwegian, Finnish and Icelandic pages were read for this. Sweden's page did not render, so its part rests on the EU-level documents.
  • Questionnaires, diaries and electronic reported outcomes are named as patient-facing documents. The Commission's Q&A (item 1.24) says those tied to the primary or secondary endpoints go with the protocol in Part I, and for a trial in several countries they go in English. Translations belong in Part II. It also says the regulation gives no legal basis to require all such documents, or their translation, in Part II. Subject information and consent forms are separate and sit in Part II.
  • The Q&A does not say how ethics committees assess these documents. That is left to national practice, and no Nordic page stating it was found, except as noted for Norway.

What this means for an electronic assessment

Inference, not an authority statement: in Norway an endpoint-linked electronic questionnaire falls under the Part I material the authority names, so screens in Norwegian or English are reviewed there. In the other countries no authority page that could be read says whether screens, app text and instructions are expected in the application at all. The questions below are worth putting to the authority or ethics committee before translating anything.

  • Are electronic assessment screens and instructions reviewed as part of the application, and in which part?
  • Must a translated version be submitted, or is the English version enough for the protocol part?
  • Is a wording change after approval a substantial modification, and who decides?
  • Are approved instrument translations accepted as they are, or does the committee expect to see the wording?
  • Does the committee want screenshots or a live demonstration?

Personal data in Denmark

The Danish Data Protection Agency's page on research says the Danish Data Protection Act's research provision can be the legal basis for processing sensitive data in statistical or scientific studies without consent as defined by the GDPR. It also says clinical drug trials are governed by the Medicines Act and that this provision cannot be relied on for them, and that transfers outside the area covered by the GDPR under the research provision need the agency's prior approval, with the United States given as an example. This was read through a page summary only, so read the original before relying on it. No statement from the agency was found about electronic assessment providers, standard contractual clauses or the EU–US Data Privacy Framework.

National patient-reported outcome programmes

These matter for registries and routine care more than for a trial, but they shape which instruments are already translated and in use.

  • Denmark. A national framework for patient-reported outcomes was agreed in 2016 by the health ministry and the regions and municipalities, led by the health data agency, with the clinical quality databases and patient organisations named as participants. Whether it is current was not checked.
  • Sweden. A 2021 publication from the municipalities and regions association says over 85% of national quality registries collect patient-reported measures, with more than 150 scales in use, and notes that some instruments need a licence that may carry a fee.
  • Norway. A centre in Bergen advises the national medical quality registries on choosing validated measures.
  • Finland and Iceland. Not researched.

No Nordic authority guidance written specifically for clinical outcome assessment operations was found.

What adds work in each language

Inference from the sources above, not an authority position.

StageWhat the Nordic setting adds
Choosing instrumentsCheck for an approved version in each language, and that its owner allows an electronic version. Finland-Swedish is a separate version from Swedish. Norwegian has two written standards, and the sources read do not say which is approved.
ApplicationEndpoint-linked electronic assessments with the protocol; subject information in the national language; what the authority wants beyond that varies, and is a question to ask.
TranslationNeeded only where no approved version exists; otherwise translate only study-specific text such as instructions, alerts and navigation. A new translation needs testing of the wording with participants.
Build and reviewLonger translated text on small screens; screen proofreading in each language; approval from the copyright holder where required.
Change controlAny wording change after sign-off needs the developer's or copyright holder's agreement, and may need a fresh submission if participant material changes.

Open items

Not found, and worth asking: language rules for participant documents in Denmark, Sweden and Iceland; whether any Nordic ethics committee reviews electronic assessment screens and app text; the contents of the Danish ethics committees' Part II guidance; how Norway and Iceland apply the regulation under the EEA Agreement; Datatilsynet's position on US providers; Nordic coverage of four of the six instruments above; the current state of Denmark's national programme.
