Clinical outcome assessment types, and what each asks of an eCOA study

A clinical outcome assessment is a way of measuring how a person feels, functions or survives, where the answer comes from a person and not from a laboratory or a scan. Which of four types it is decides who answers, on what device, with what training, and which records the study has to keep. Get the type wrong and every later step is built on it.

Reference · sources checked 2026-11-14

Boundary. A reference for planning, built from regulator and consensus documents listed in the source register. It is not regulatory advice and does not say which instrument suits your study or whether an endpoint will be accepted. Where a source was only partly read, the register says so.

The four types

The FDA's patient-focused drug development glossary gives four types. The deciding question is who reports, not what the instrument is called. A final FDA guidance from October 2025 sits behind the type system, and a secondary summary reports that it keeps all four. The FDA glossary itself is due an update once the fourth guidance is final.

TypeWho reportsPlain-language testWhat it asks of an eCOA study
Patient-reported outcome (PRO)The participant, about their own healthThe answer is recorded as given, with nobody interpreting it.Whose device and who supplies it; a licensed version in every language; a schedule that matches the recall period; a plan for late and missing entries.
Observer-reported outcome (ObsRO)A parent, caregiver or other non-professional, about what they can seeReports signs or behaviours that need no medical judgement.Who counts as the observer and what happens if someone else reports; the observer's training and language; a record of who reported each entry.
Clinician-reported outcome (ClinRO)A trained health professional using clinical judgementNeeds professional training to interpret what is seen.Rater qualification and training before the first rating; consistency between raters and sites; where the rating is first recorded.
Performance outcome (PerfO)The participant does a standard task; the result is measured or scoredA task under set conditions, scored by an administrator or by the task itself.Standard conditions and equipment; a script so every site gives the same instructions; device calibration where a device measures the task.

A single instrument can sit in more than one place in a study. A symptom diary a caregiver completes for a child is an observer report; the same questions completed by the child are a participant report. Treat them as different assessments, with their own validation and translation.

Two questions before choosing an instrument

The FDA glossary defines the concept of interest as what the assessment is meant to measure, and the context of use as how its scores will be used as the basis for an endpoint. An instrument is fit for purpose for one concept in one context, not in general. The FDA's October 2025 guidance is about selecting, developing or modifying an assessment on that basis. The COA type finder records both, with the reporter and the mode, for each assessment in a study.

From paper to electronic

Moving an existing instrument to a screen is a change that needs to be faithful to the original. The ISPOR task force's 2023 update says that where the existing evidence is adequate and the recognised migration practices are followed, new comparability testing is not needed, including for mixed modes such as participants using their own phones. Only the abstract was readable. The 2024 consortium paper gathers the migration practices in one place and marks which are backed by evidence and which are consensus. Its condensed version sets out the rules that matter most on screen: one item per screen, no pre-selected answers, and every item either answered or deliberately skipped.

Languages

For participant-reported measures the standard method is the ISPOR translation process of 2005. For the other three types a 2020 paper adapts it, mainly in how the wording is tested with people. The 2025 ISOQOL paper on electronic assessments recommends running translation and the electronic build together, checking early how longer translated text fits the screens, having the language provider proofread the screens in every language, and allowing at least two review rounds. Practical planning figures from vendors and language providers are in the lead-time planner, with the reservation that they are not benchmarks.

Testing before the first participant

The consortium's recommendations on user acceptance testing say the sponsor leads it, it follows written scripts, it is done outside the live environment, and the plan, the findings log, the summary report and the signed approval are kept as study records. The EMA guideline on computerised systems adds that the trial-specific configuration, not only the product, needs validating, and its Annex 5 covers the specifics of provisioned and own devices.

What the regulators have said

  • EMA, 2005. Quality-of-life measures are an optional, sponsor-driven endpoint. They need a validated instrument, a pre-specified testing order, a pre-defined meaningful difference and a plan for missing data. Translation and cultural validation count towards the strength of the evidence.
  • EMA, oncology appendix, 2016. Pre-specify the PRO hypotheses, show that paper and electronic give equivalent data, offer a paper alternative and prevent missing data in preference to repairing it afterwards.
  • EMA, patient experience data, draft. Asks developers to engage regulators early on when patient experience data will support a development programme. Consultation closed 2026-01-31 and no final version was found.
  • ICH E6(R3), in force in the EU from 2025-07-23. Computerised systems must be fit for purpose with risk-based validation, and investigators are expected to review participant-reported data when relevant.
  • FDA, October 2024. Questions and answers on electronic systems in clinical investigations: risk-based validation, audit trails, and that participant-reported data should identify the participant as the originator.

After the data are collected

ICH E9(R1) separates events that happen after randomisation, such as stopping treatment or taking rescue medicine, from missing data. Each event needs a pre-specified strategy, and the choice changes what a questionnaire score means. A 2020 paper applies this to patient-reported quality of life. The practical consequence for eCOA planning is to decide, before the build, what the system records about treatment status and why an entry is missing. CDISC publishes supplements for representing instruments in submission datasets; its clearance to publish a supplement is not permission to use the instrument.
