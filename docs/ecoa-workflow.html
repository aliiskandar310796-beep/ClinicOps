eCOA workflow map, from decision to close-out

Twelve steps, numbered 0 to 11, that an electronic outcome-assessment study passes through: what has to be settled at each, what it leaves behind, and which tool on this site helps. It is provider-neutral: it describes the work, not any one vendor's platform.

Rules as of 16 November 2026 · last verified against the sources named below · next review by 14 February 2027.

Reference · ClinicOps synthesis of published guidance, not a standard · sources checked 2026-11-16

Boundary. A ClinicOps synthesis of published guidance and consensus papers, not a standard and not a checklist that makes a study compliant. It makes no claim about how any particular eCOA platform is configured, and the order shown is the usual one, not a rule: licensing and translation start early and overlap the build, and steps 9 and 10 run side by side after go-live. The ISPOR validation task force frames validation as a life cycle that runs from requirements to decommissioning, which is why the map ends where it does.

Running through every stage

  • Change management. A wording or schedule change after sign-off can need the instrument developer's or copyright holder's agreement, and for some changes new evidence. The 2025 translation paper says changes during implementation should be discussed with developers and copyright holders. Record every change, who approved it and what it touched; step 10 is where that record lives. A change to source text also reaches every language version and deliverable that carries it, so list them: the change surface mapper has an optional language dimension for this.
  • Oversight. A sponsor stays answerable for what a provider does on its behalf. The EMA guideline on computerised systems and electronic data in clinical trials expects clear written agreements between the parties and keeps responsibility for data integrity with the sponsor or institution. Agree at step 0 what will be reviewed and how often, and keep what was seen.
  • Risk. Keep one register. The usual sources are a licence not yet granted, a language version still in review, a late specification change, devices or own-device access, and a testing window squeezed by earlier delays. Every risk gets an owner and a date.
  • Education. A sponsor choosing electronic collection for the first time needs the same explanation of equivalence, validation and licensing that an experienced team already has. Say what the study will and will not be able to show.
  • Communication. Sponsor, provider, translation, copyright holders, sites and, where relevant, patient representatives each need to know what is decided and what is waiting on them. The workbench keeps decisions in one place so that a question has a single answer.

What good looks like at the end

Anyone running the study can say, for each assessment: who answers, on what, in which language, when, what happens if they do not, and who may change it. The post-project review adds what the plan got wrong, which is more useful to the next study than any template.

Free templates

Blank files, no sign-up and no email address asked. Each carries its version, date, sources and licence inside the file. They are templates, not compliance determinations.

  • eCOA UAT script skeleton, findings log and approval form for step 6 (XLSX, version 1.0, 2026-11-16, CC BY 4.0). It adapts the best-practice paper on user acceptance testing by Gordon et al. (ePRO Consortium and PRO Consortium, 2022; CC BY 4.0): reorganised and shortened in ClinicOps wording, with example steps added, and not endorsed by the authors. Rules from the paper are built in: UAT is not run in Production, the provider's staff do not execute it, a failed step fails the whole test case, all findings go in one log, and a deferred item needs both parties' agreement and a communication plan. The sponsor or its designee still writes and approves the real scripts.
  • Terminology governance template for step 4 (one printed page, XLSX; CSV copy; version 1.0, 2026-11-16, CC BY 4.0, original ClinicOps work). It records who owns and approves a term and what a change touches. As an example of a stated expectation it cites one regulator page: the Danish Medicines Agency lists consistent terminology among the standards it expects of the Danish text of product information for centrally authorised medicines. That page is about medicines, not devices or trial material.
